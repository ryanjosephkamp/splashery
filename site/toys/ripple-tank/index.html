About this toy

A ripple tank shows how waves spread, bend round edges and add up. This one solves the real two-dimensional wave equation on a grid of 216 × 216 cells (finite differences, with a time step small enough to stay stable), drawn as a fine surface of splats lifted by the water's height and lit so the crests catch the light. Sloping beaches at the edges soak the waves up, as in a real tank.

Through two slits, the waves add where their paths differ by whole wavelengths, so bright lines fan out at the angles d·sin θ = m·λ gives; the dotted lines mark them. Through one slit, the beam spreads wider as the slit gets narrower. The bars on the back wall show how strong the waves are along the far side. The numbers are in the Toy tab.

What it simplifies: real ripples on shallow water change speed a little with their wavelength; here every wavelength moves at the speed you set. The heights are exaggerated and the motion is four times slower than real.

Is it right?

The ripple tank solves the two-dimensional wave equation on a grid and draws the water's height. Through two slits its bright fringes land where d·sin θ = m·λ says, and a single slit's beam spreads wider as the slit narrows, both measured from the simulation itself. The water is shown slowed down and with its heights exaggerated, and every wavelength travels at the one speed set. Checked October 5, 2026.

  1. Correct The water follows the wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = c²∇²u, solved by finite differences.

    Each step updates every cell from its four neighbors with the standard second-order centered scheme (the five-point Laplacian); a damping term is added only in the beaches at the edges.

    The test checks that a dipper's crests are λ = c / f apart (within 2%).

    • Wave equation, Wikipedia: In two dimensions u_tt = c²(u_xx + u_yy); the wave equation describes mechanical waves such as water waves and sound waves, and light.

  2. Correct The time step is small enough for the scheme to stay stable.

    Δt is set so the Courant number c·Δt/Δx is 0.5 at the fastest speed the controls allow, so even the sum over both directions (2 × 0.5) stays at the limit of 1.

    The test runs 6,000 steps with a source and a barrier and checks the water stays bounded.

    • Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition, Wikipedia: In two dimensions C = u_x Δt/Δx + u_y Δt/Δy ≤ C_max; for an explicit (time-marching) solver typically C_max = 1.

  3. Correct The edges absorb the waves, like the sloping beaches of a real ripple tank.

    A border 5 cm wide damps the water, gently at its inner edge and more toward the wall (as the square of the depth into it), so a ring crosses it and dies instead of bouncing back.

    After a pebble's rings reach the edges, under 1% of their energy is left in the tank.

    • Ripple tank, Wikipedia: All the basic properties of waves, including reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction, can be demonstrated; the ripples show up as shadows on the screen underneath.

  4. Close Through two slits d apart, the bright fringes are at angles where d·sin θ = m·λ.

    The test runs a plane wave through two slits in a large tank and measures the time-averaged strength of the water along an arc 52 cm from the slits, then finds its peaks.

    For d = 10 and 12 cm and λ = 4 cm the first bright fringes land within 0.6° of the law; the second, at wide angles, within 4°, pulled in by each slit's own spreading.

    • Double-slit experiment, Wikipedia: The interference fringe maxima occur at angles d sin θ = nλ, n = 0, 1, 2, … (bright bands where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths).

  5. Close A single slit's beam spreads wider as the slit narrows, with its first dark fringe at sin θ = λ / a.

    The test measures the beam's half width for slits 12, 8 and 5 cm wide, and the first dark fringe for the 8 cm slit.

    Each narrower slit's beam is at least 20% wider; the 8 cm slit's first dark fringe is within 1.5° of 30°.

    • Double-slit experiment, Wikipedia: A single slit of width b gives an intensity proportional to sinc²(π b sin θ / λ), which is first zero where b sin θ = λ.

  6. Correct The dotted lines mark where the two paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths.

    For each cell past the slits (or the two dippers) the toy works out the difference of its distances to the two and dots the cells where it is a multiple of λ; far away these lines run at the angles d·sin θ = m·λ gives.

    • Double-slit experiment, Wikipedia: Bright bands appear where the path difference is an integral number of wavelengths.

Where it simplifies

  • Real ripples on shallow water are dispersive (their speed changes a little with the wavelength, and surface tension matters for short ones); here every wavelength moves at the speed set by the slider.
  • The motion is shown four times slower than real, and the heights are exaggerated so the crests can be seen.
  • The light and shade are worked out from the water's slope and curvature for the picture; they are not a ray-traced image of a lamp through water.
  • The dippers are idealized: each holds a small disc of water to a sine wave.
