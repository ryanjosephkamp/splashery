The real Earth labs

Tap to fly to Everest, the Grand Canyon or Hawaii and back. Move the Sun to see city lights.

About this toy

The real Earth from NASA and NOAA maps: colors from NASA's Blue Marble (a cloud-free mosaic from the Terra satellite), heights from NOAA's ETOPO 2022, and city lights from NASA's Black Marble. The oceans lie flat at sea level. Each splat sits at its height and leans with its ground, so the sun lights the slopes facing it.

The relief is exaggerated twenty times by default (Everest, 8.8 km high, is less than a thousandth of Earth's width); pick True height in the Toy tab to see it as it is. Earth turns once every 23 hours 56 minutes; here an hour passes each second. Close up, the relief eases to at most twice its height. There are no clouds or shadows.

Is it right?

Earth drawn from real maps: every splat sits at its place's measured height and has its place's measured color, and each named feature is where the planetary gazetteer puts it. The relief, the turning speed and the lighting are simplified, and each simplification is labeled in the toy. Checked October 5, 2026.

Where it simplifies

  • The relief is exaggerated by default (ten or twenty times; Pluto, Ceres and Vesta are drawn at true height), as chosen and labeled in the Toy tab; close up during a fly it eases to at most twice its height. True height is one choice away.
  • The world turns thousands of times faster than real (labeled in the Toy tab: an hour or a day each second).
  • North is always up and the sun stays near the equator's plane: the axial tilt and the seasons are left out.
  • The sun shades each slope by the cosine of its angle to the sun (Lambert's law) and leaves the night side dark, but nothing casts a shadow, and real surfaces scatter light in more complicated ways.
  • The global map has about 1,000 by 500 heights and 2,000 by 1,000 colors; only the patches round the named features are sharper.
  • The oceans are drawn flat at sea level; there are no clouds; the blue rim of air is a drawn glow.
  • The city lights are NASA's 2016 composite of cloud-free nights, not one night.
