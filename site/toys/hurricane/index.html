About this toy

Hurricane Polo grew over the warm eastern Pacific, off the coast of Mexico, in September 2026. In the 24 hours from September 21 to 22 its strongest winds rose from 70 to 155 knots (about 290 kilometers an hour), a burst forecasters call rapid intensification, and a clear eye formed in its center.

The clouds are built from infrared pictures from NOAA's GOES-East satellite at the start and end of that day: colder cloud tops are higher, so each cloud stands as tall as its temperature says. They turn counterclockwise around the eye, faster near the center, with rain falling and low winds spiraling in. Tap it to play the day: the storm follows its best track from the National Hurricane Center, the early ragged clouds give way to the later ones, and it spins faster as it strengthens. The two pictures are real; the change between them is a blend.

Sources and credits

Built in your browser from a recipe, with the files credited below. Its recipe is in src/packs/geo.js.

  • Clouds: GOES-East ABI band 13 (clean infrared), through NASA GIBS by NOAA NESDIS (imagery served by NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services), Public domain.
  • Track: Best track of Hurricane Polo (EP17, 2026) by NOAA National Hurricane Center, Public domain.
  • Map: Blue Marble: Next Generation (true color), through NASA GIBS by NASA Earth Observatory, Public domain.
  • Relief: ETOPO1 Global Relief Model by NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, Public domain.
