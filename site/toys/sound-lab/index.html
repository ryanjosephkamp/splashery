About this toy

Sound is air pressure going up and down, and this bench shows it four ways. The oscilloscope draws the wave over a few thousandths of a second. The spectrum splits it into frequencies and names the loudest. The spectrogram keeps eight seconds of spectra side by side: a whistle is a bright line, a clap a column. The level is in decibels below full scale (dBFS).

Tap to play the tone generator, a sine, square, saw or noise from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, through the speaker when the site's sound is on. Two close tones beat: 440 and 443 Hz swell and fade three times a second. A square wave's extra lines are its odd harmonics. Tap “Use my microphone” to see your voice instead; the sound goes only to the screen. The dB SPL figure is an uncalibrated estimate that assumes a typical phone microphone. The metronome clicks once a beat as it swings from side to side.

Is it right?

The Sound lab measures sound: a spectrogram, the spectrum with its loudest frequency, an oscilloscope and a level in dBFS, from the microphone's samples or from the tone generator's own signal, computed exactly as the speaker plays it. The measurements are real and tested on known tones; the sound pressure level in dB SPL is an uncalibrated estimate. Checked October 5, 2026.

  1. Correct The spectrum's peak is the frequency of the tone being played or heard.

    A 4096-sample Hann-windowed FFT (the song landscape's), scaled so a full-scale sine reads 0 dB, with the peak refined by a parabola through the loudest bin and its neighbors.

    Within 0.5% from 100 Hz to 12 kHz; the in-page test checks 440 Hz, 3 kHz and a 1 kHz microphone signal.

    • Spectrogram, Wikipedia: Windowing the signal and taking the squared magnitude of its short-time Fourier transform gives the spectrum at each moment.

  2. Correct A square wave has only odd harmonics, at 1/3, 1/5 … of the fundamental's amplitude.

    The generator's square wave is analyzed like any other sound; its spectrum shows lines at 3f and 5f about 9.5 and 14 dB down and none at 2f.

    • Square wave, Wikipedia: An ideal square wave contains only odd-integer harmonic frequencies, the nth with amplitude 1/n of the fundamental.

  3. Correct Two tones close in frequency beat at the difference of their frequencies.

    The second tone adds to the first; the level and the spectrogram swell and fade |f1 − f2| times a second.

    Two tones 3 Hz apart are closer than one bin of the spectrum (11.7 Hz), so they show as one peak that pulses, as they would on any analyzer of that resolution.

    • Beat (acoustics), Wikipedia: An interference pattern between two sounds of slightly different frequencies, perceived as a periodic variation in volume, the rate of which is the difference of the two frequencies.

  4. Correct The level is the RMS of the last 50 ms in decibels below full scale (dBFS): a full-scale sine reads −3 dB.

    20 log10 of the RMS of the samples.

    • DBFS, Wikipedia: In the convention where the RMS value of a full-scale square wave is 0 dB, a sine wave cannot exist at a larger RMS value than −3 dB without clipping.

  5. Simplified The dB SPL figure is about dBFS + 120.

    A typical digital MEMS microphone has a sensitivity of −26 dBFS at 94 dB SPL, so full scale is about 120 dB SPL. The Toy tab can change the 120.

    Browsers and phones apply their own gain (the page turns off automatic gain control where the browser allows), so the figure is an estimate, labeled uncalibrated on screen.

    • Understanding Microphone Sensitivity (Analog Dialogue 46-05), Analog Devices: If a digital microphone's maximum SPL is 120 dB, its sensitivity is −26 dBFS (94 dB − 120 dB); typical MEMS sensitivity is −26 dBFS.

  6. Simplified The metronome clicks once a beat at the tempo set.

    Clicks are scheduled on the audio clock at (k + ½) × 60 / bpm seconds; the pendulum's angle is A sin(π t bpm / 60), one swing a beat.

    • Metronome, Wikipedia: A device that produces a sound at a regular interval, set in beats per minute.

Where it simplifies

  • The click falls at each end of the pendulum's swing. A mechanical metronome's escapement ticks at a set angle of its swing, which differs from model to model.
  • The dB SPL figure is uncalibrated and unweighted (no A weighting).
  • The speaker's cone moves with the signal at the frame's moment, scaled up so it shows; at audio frequencies a real cone's motion is far too fast and small to see.
  • Screens show the generator's signal as computed here; the speaker plays the browser's oscillators, whose square and saw waves are band-limited (they differ only above the highest harmonic the sample rate can carry).
