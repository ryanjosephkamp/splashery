About this toy

Every element as a real sample: lumps of metal, crystals, liquids and glowing gases in sealed glass. Each sample is an open photo of the real thing, cut out and raised in 3D by a depth model. The front is real; the depth is an estimate, and the back of a lifted sample is made up from its own colors.

Tap a tile and its sample lifts out and turns toward you, with its facts from PubChem: mass, group and period, state, density, melting and boiling points, discovery and uses. Tap the sample to turn it, or its tile to set it back. 26 elements have no photo of a real sample, as the heaviest exist only a few atoms at a time; their hatched tiles show a stand-in. Photos: Images of Elements (CC BY 3.0) and Wikimedia Commons.

Is it right?

The toy shows each element's facts (atomic mass, group and period, state at room temperature, density, melting and boiling points, discovery and one or two uses) and, for 92 elements, a photo of a real sample raised in 3D. The facts are PubChem's, copied by a build tool and checked against a saved copy of PubChem's table for all 118 elements; each use is our own short sentence backed by the words of Jefferson Lab or Los Alamos National Laboratory as PubChem quotes them. The samples' fronts are real photos; their depth is a depth model's estimate and their backs are invented. Checked October 5, 2026.

  1. Correct All 118 elements are in the standard 18-column layout, with the right atomic numbers, symbols and names.

    The facts table is written from PubChem's periodic table by tools/rel-facts.mjs; the tile places come from the period lengths (2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32), with the lanthanoids and actinoids in two rows below.

    • Periodic Table of Elements, PubChem (NCBI, U.S. National Library of Medicine): The table of the 118 elements with their atomic numbers, symbols, names and group blocks (served as JSON at https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/periodictable/JSON).
    • Periodic Table of the Elements, IUPAC: The standard layout of the 118 named elements; the symbols agree with the table the Periodic table toy uses (src/chem/periodic.js, from NIST and IUPAC).

  2. Correct Atomic mass, state at room temperature, density, melting and boiling points, year of discovery and category are PubChem's values.

    Each value is copied from PubChem's table at build time; the test compares every one of them for all 118 elements with the saved snapshot (tools/rel-reference.json), and ten elements' values typed in by hand from their PubChem element pages.

    Temperatures are shown in °C (K − 273.15, rounded to 0.1). A whole-number mass is the mass number of the longest-lived isotope (shown in brackets). Gas densities are shown per liter.

    • PubChem periodic table (JSON), PubChem (NCBI): Columns AtomicMass, StandardState, MeltingPoint (K), BoilingPoint (K), Density (g/cm³), GroupBlock and YearDiscovered for each element.
    • Iron, PubChem (NCBI): Atomic mass 55.84; density 7.874 g/cm³; melting point 1811 K; boiling point 3134 K; known since ancient times.

  3. Correct Each use shown is a real use of the element.

    Every use is a short sentence of our own with an exact quote from the element's 'Uses' section on PubChem (from Jefferson Lab's 'It's Elemental' or Los Alamos National Laboratory's periodic table); the build stops if a quote is not in its source, and the test checks each quote against the saved source sentence.

    A few uses the sources give are older (radium paint on dials, red phosphors in color TV screens); they are worded in the past tense or as the sources say. Uses the sources don't mention (neodymium magnets, for one) are left out rather than added without a source.

    • It's Elemental, Jefferson Lab, U.S. Department of Energy: For example, iron: 'Huge amounts of iron are used to make steel, an alloy of iron and carbon.'.
    • Periodic Table of Elements, Los Alamos National Laboratory, U.S. Department of Energy: For example, americium: 'americium has been employed to gauge glass thickness to help create flat glass' (quoted on PubChem's element page).

  4. Correct Group and period follow the element's place in the table.

    The period is the row; the group is the column of the 18-column table. Lanthanoids and actinoids are shown as 'Lanthanoid' or 'Actinoid' with their period, not given a group number.

    Which elements sit in group 3 (La and Ac, or Lu and Lr) is debated; the toy follows the common layout and gives no group number to the f-block.

    • Periodic Table of the Elements, IUPAC: Groups 1 to 18 and periods 1 to 7, with the f-block shown below the table.

  5. Correct Each sample is a photo of the real element; an element with no such photo says why and shows a stand-in picture, said plainly to be one.

    92 photos of real samples (Images of Elements, CC BY 3.0; Wikimedia Commons, public domain, CC BY or CC BY-SA), each license read on its live page. 26 elements have none: the sources show only illustrations (polonium, astatine, radon, francium, mendelevium to lawrencium), the only photos found aren't openly licensed (actinium, thorium, curium), or no visible amount has ever been made (fermium and everything from rutherfordium on). Each of those shows a stand-in from Wikimedia Commons: the person, place or flag it is named for, or a mineral that holds traces of it; the facts list says 'Shown instead' before it.

    Some samples are compounds or mixtures, said in their description: iron is a fragment of an iron meteorite (about 92% iron), radium is plated on copper foil, protactinium-233 sits on a support. Sodium and others are kept under oil or argon, and the gases are shown glowing in sealed vials. Promethium's picture is the first two buttons of promethium-147 metal from a U.S. Atomic Energy Commission report, a real sample. Dubnium's stand-in is Dubna's coat of arms: dubnium is named for Dubna, Russia, not Dublin.

    • Images of Elements, Jumk.de Webprojects: The images are licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 Unported License, unless otherwise noted; pages like promethium's say 'This is only an illustration'.

Where it simplifies

  • The 3D samples are photos given depth by a depth model (Depth Anything V2 Small), which judges from one photo how far away each part is: the front is the real photo, the depth an estimate, and the back of a lifted sample made up from its own colors, since no photo saw it.
  • Samples are not to scale: each fills its tile, whether it is a 6 kg sphere (neptunium) or 1.7 micrograms (berkelium); the descriptions give the real sizes where the sources do.
  • PubChem gives some values under special conditions: helium freezes only under pressure (the toy says so), arsenic sublimes at normal pressure (the toy says so), and carbon's listed melting point is for graphite under pressure.
