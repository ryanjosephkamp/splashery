How 3D Gaussian splats work

A soft colored blob, a lot of them, and a recipe: the whole idea, with pictures.

One splat is a soft, colored blob

A 3D Gaussian splat is the smallest piece of every toy here. Think of a tiny ball of colored fog. It has a center (where it sits in space), a size in each direction (so it can be a ball, a disc or a long grain of rice) with a tilt, a color and an opacity. Its edge isn't sharp: it fades out smoothly, the way a drop of ink fades into wet paper. Mathematicians call that fade a Gaussian, which is where the name comes from.

Hundreds of thousands of them make a picture

One splat is hardly anything. But a scan of a strawberry is made of about 450,000 of them, each a little different: some small and dark where the seeds sit, some wide and red on the smooth skin. Packed together they overlap, and your eye can't see where one stops and the next begins. What you see is the berry.

Try it: open the strawberry and pinch in close. The fruit turns into a cloud of soft blobs, and zooming back out makes it solid again.

Sorted, then blended

To draw a frame, the computer does two things with all those splats. First it sorts them by distance from you, farthest first. Then it paints them one after another, and each one is see-through, so it only tints what is already there. A near splat covers a far one, and where they overlap their colors mix. Do that sixty times a second and the picture can move.

That is the whole trick, and it is why splats look so soft and real: no hard polygons, no textures, just blended color. It also means every splat is free to move on its own, which is what makes toys that peel, ripple, shatter and come back together possible.

Where the splats come from

There are two kinds of toys on Splashery.

  • Captured toys are scans. Someone walked around a real strawberry or cactus with a camera, a program worked out the splats that would reproduce those photos, and Splashery shows the result. Each scan's author and license are in the credits.
  • Recipe toys are made from a short description. A recipe says what a toy is built from (a cone for the tower, a box for each sail), what color each part is, and what moves and how. Your browser reads the recipe and makes the splats when you pick the toy, so a toy costs a few thousand characters instead of megabytes.

Because a recipe is plain text, anyone can write one. The Tinkerer's Manual teaches it from the math of one splat up to your own toys, and Writing toy recipes is the reference.

Why make toys from splats?

Splats are usually something you watch: a scan you orbit and then close. Here they are something you play with. Because each splat is separate, a toy can peel like a grape, pour like water or ring like a piano, and the same recipes run atoms, planets and games. And since the whole thing is plain web code, it works on a phone with nothing to install.
